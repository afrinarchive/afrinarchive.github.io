Basic Information about Yazi Baĝ

Source: Tirej Afrin

Etymology: The name is derived from the Kurdish word Bax meaning "wooded place" and the Turkish word Yazi meaning "summer or face". It was Arabized to El-Kurûm due to the abundance of vine shrubs there.

Foundation Date/Period: Approximately 250 years

Hills: Çiyayê Parse, Barse Dax

I. Summary from TirejAfrin Site (English) of Yazi Baĝ

Source: https://www.tirejafrin.com/site/kura%20afrin%20%20sheran%20-%20yaze%20bax.htm

It is stated in the book جبل الكرد (عفرين) دراسة جغرافية Çiyayê Kurmênc (Efrîn): A Geographical Study regarding Yazibax Yazî Baxî El-Kurûm: 315 inhabitants, 215 hectares, 12 km, 660 m.

Bax is a Kurdish name meaning "wooded place" and Yazi means "the summer or the face" in Turkish. The name was Arabized to El-Kurûm due to the abundance of vine shrubs in it.

It is a small village located on the northern slope of Çiyayê Parse. It abuts the Turkish border on its northern side.

It is stated in the book عفرين .... نهرها وروابيها الخضراء Efrîn... Her River and Her Green Hills that Yazibax is a village in Çiyayê Kurmênc following the subdistrict of Şeran, in the region of Efrîn, governorate of Heleb. It is a small village located in the eastern part of the Çiyayê Kurmênc massif on the northwestern slope of the grooved limestone mountain of Barse Dax. Its soil is clay, and pastures are spread on the slopes. It is 12 km away from the town of Şeran towards the northeast and abuts the current Turkish border at a distance of 300 m on its northern side.

It is bordered to the north by a plain planted with fruit trees. It is bordered to the south by Çiyayê Barse which is planted with forest trees and vineyards, and the village of Me'rîn. It is bordered to the east by a plain, a valley, and directly by the Turkish border.

The site of the village is situated in a depression and a plain, surrounded on all sides by dense trees of fruit bearing varieties. To the west is an agriculturally fertile plain planted with fruit trees, almonds, walnuts, and some orchards irrigated by artesian wells, as well as the villages of Dîkmetaş and Qestel Cindo.

The number of its houses is about 35 houses and its age is about 250 years. Its old dwellings are stone and mud with flat wooden roofs, while the modern ones are cement and have intermingled with the old houses. The village has an electricity network, a primary school, a mosque in the center of the village, and an asphalt road connecting to the region of Ezaz via the village of Me'rîn.

The residents work in the cultivation of olives, vineyards, grains, and fruits such as apples, walnuts, and almonds. A portion of these trees are irrigated from artesian wells. They use these wells for drinking water, and these wells are numerous and abundant, leading to the availability of water there. They also raise sheep and goats.

This village is located in the eastern corner of the Efrîn region and was administratively annexed to the region of Ezaz in the year 1988.

II. Summary of Yazi Baĝ from Multi Channel

The village of Yazîbax is located in the eastern part of the Çiyayê Kurmênc massif and sits on the northwestern slope of Çiyayê Barsa. Situated at an elevation of 660 meters above sea level, the village was administratively annexed to the Ezaz district in 1988. The name of the village has Tirk origins, where Yazî translates to summer and Bax refers to a planted area, orchard, or vineyard. Because of the vast number of grapevines traditionally grown there, the village name was officially Arabized to El Kurûm, meaning the vineyards in Ereb.

Historically, the domestic architecture of Yazîbax consisted of traditional stone and mud houses featuring flat wooden roofs. Over time, modern cement houses were built and now intermingle with the older structures. During a tour of the older neighborhoods, residents point out historical features that provide a glimpse into the timeline of the village. A local farmer named Mistefa shows an ancient mulberry tree that his grandfather planted, estimating it to be around 150 to 200 years old. He also showcases a water well dug by his father in 1944. Furthermore, part of an old stable on his property bears an inscription dating it to the Islamic year 1325, which is roughly 115 years ago.

Another prominent historical feature in Yazîbax is an antique olive press and grain mill owned by an eighty year old resident named Ebû Rifet. The mill relies on large black stones that were traditionally rotated by horses or cows to grind wheat into bulgur and crush olives for oil. Although this stone mill has been out of operation for about two decades, it stands as a testament to the traditional agricultural practices that predated modern machinery and commercial transport to nearby Ezaz.

Agriculture remains the primary economic lifeline for the residents of Yazîbax. The fertile land and surrounding plains are densely planted with olive groves, cherry orchards, walnut trees, apple orchards, and vineyards. Water is sourced from artesian wells and traditional structures known as Cibabên Erebî which collect winter rainwater. The agricultural lifestyle shapes the daily routines of the villagers, particularly the women who traditionally prepare apricot jam and grape molasses. The grape molasses is made by boiling the grapes in large cauldrons and adding a special earth called Cîcan to sweeten and clarify the syrup.

Socially, the people of Yazîbax describe themselves as modest and welcoming to strangers. Interestingly, their cultural and culinary preferences reflect their geographical positioning. When discussing local food, residents note that they do not favor the sour dishes typical of Heleb cuisine. Instead, they feel a closer culinary affinity to Tirk traditions, particularly favoring dishes like kebabs. Today, despite facing modern agricultural challenges such as the high cost of fuel and medicine for their livestock, the villagers remain deeply tied to their land and their heritage.

II. Summary of Yazi Baĝ from Multi Channel 2

The village of Yazî Bax is located in northern Syria. It formerly belonged to the Şera subdistrict in the Efrîn region before being administratively reassigned to Ezaz just prior to the Syrian revolution. The village sits exactly north of Çiyayê Barsa, near the border crossing of Bab el Selame and the town of Sicû, and is located only a few kilometers from Kîlîs in Turkey. To its west lies Qestel Cindo. The name Yazî Bax means plain and vineyard in the Turkish language. The government later Arabized the name to Kurûm, meaning vineyards, and the local mosque is accordingly named the Mosque of the Vineyards.

Historical artifacts found in Çiyayê Barsa indicate human settlement in the area during the Roman and Byzantine periods. However, the modern village was founded between 1700 and 1750 AD. According to local elders, the village was established by a man named Hisên Bekîr who migrated from Meraş in Turkey. The community initially included members of the Reşwan tribe. Over centuries of intermarriage, the population consolidated into a unified Turkmen identity. Today, the residents are mostly of Turkmen descent with some Arab origins, and they speak Arabic, Turkish, and Kurdish in their daily lives. The primary families inhabiting the village include Îbîş, Hec Yasîn, Hemoş, Xelo, Bisêk, Qere Xelîl, Etono, Reşo, Bekîr, and Misto.

Economically, the villagers have historically relied on agriculture, cultivating olives, wheat, and various fruits. The village houses an old olive press built in 1986 and is characterized by large mulberry trees planted by ancestors to provide shade for social gatherings. Because of limited local opportunities, many young residents have recently migrated to Heleb or Turkey to work in industrial trades, leaving mostly the elderly in the village. Education levels have historically been very low, especially for women. The oldest standing structures in the village are a school and a mosque, both built in 1952.

A ninety year old resident named Hemoş shared his memories of the local resistance against France during his childhood. He recalled that an uprising began against the French forces, led by a figure from Turkey. The poorly armed locals, possessing only basic rifles, faced three hundred French soldiers supported by aircraft and tanks advancing from Ezaz. The severe imbalance of power forced the villagers to flee to Turkey for three months. During their absence, pro French feudal lords confiscated the village harvest. Once the French authorities issued a pardon, the farmers returned to their lands. Hemoş also recounted a personal tragedy from forty years ago, when the wife of a farmhand mistakenly administered battery acid instead of medicine into his eyes, leading to his permanent blindness despite multiple surgeries in Şam.
