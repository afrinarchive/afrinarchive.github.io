I. Summary from TirejAfrin Site (English) of Elcara - Mabeta

Source: https://www.tirejafrin.com/site/kura%20afrin%20%20%20mebetli%20-%20elcara.htm

The following states in the book جبل الكرد (عفرين) دراسة جغرافية Çiyayê Kurmênc (Efrîn): A Geographical Study by د. محمد عبدو علي Dr. Mihemed Ebdo Elî:

Elcara, Elî Caro / 226 inhabitants - 13km - 670m / :

The origin of its name is 'El Jar', meaning "Ali the slender," or more correctly "Ali son of the slender one." It is a small village located on a plateau furrowed by streams in all directions.

The following states in the book عفرين .... نهرها وروابيها الخضراء Efrîn... Her River and Her Green Hills by the writer عبدالرحمن محمد Ebdulrehman Mihemed from the village of Qetme:

Elî Caro: A village in Çiyayê Kurmênc, administratively belonging to the Mabeta district, Efrîn region, Heleb governorate.

It is a small village located in the central part of Çiyayê Kurmênc, on the western slope of a limestone plateau furrowed by torrential valleys from the east and west. It is 12km away from the village of Mabeta towards the southwest. Its soil is clay, with pastures and forests spread above it.

It is bordered to the north by a mountainous highland planted with olive trees and the village of Qişlê; to the south by a steep slope, a very deep torrential valley, mountainous highlands planted with olive trees and forests, and the village of Mistû Aşûr; to the west by a deep stream, mountainous highlands planted with olive trees and forests, and the village of Maxarcix; and to the east, 50m away, is the nearby village of Hemê Reco.

Its soil is clay, with pastures and forests spread above it. The number of its houses is about 35, and its age is about 350 years. Its dwellings are stone and mud with flat wooden roofs, and cement buildings have begun to overtake them.

An electricity network is available, as well as a primary school shared between the village of Qişlê, Meserê Cix, and Hemê Reco. There is a modern olive press in the village. Its residents drink from a well west of the village (500m) and from cisterns that collect rainwater in winter. There is a very small mosque in the village. A paved road connects it to the district center, passing through its center to the neighboring villages.

Its inhabitants rely on rain-fed agriculture (olives, grains, walnuts, and almonds) alongside raising sheep and goats. Among its most important families: the family of Omer Oso or Hemû Elî Caro; they were the first to inhabit the village, so the village was named after its first inhabitant.

Village Mukhtar: Remzî Heqî

II. Summary of Elcara - Mabeta from Ax û Welat

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qMOth87MVrw

The villages of Alciyara and Reca are located approximately 11 kilometers west of the district of Mabeta in the Afrin region. Although they are technically two distinct settlements, they function socially and administratively as a single entity, sharing a commune, a mosque, a cemetery, and a school. The residents are closely bound by kinship and shared history, with the physical boundaries of the villages merging over time. The economy is primarily agrarian, dependent on olive groves and fruit trees, with water traditionally sourced from local wells and springs due to the high elevation.

The origins of Alciyara are traced back to a founding figure named Eliyê Jare, also known as Elî Carû in Arabic contexts. His descendants form the core population of the village, rooted in the Biyan tribe. According to local oral history, Eliyê Jare had four sons named Hecbirîm, Os, Ehmed, and Şêxê, whose lineages constitute the primary families of Alciyara, along with the Hesûnê and Eme families. The village name itself evolved from Elî Jare to Alciyara through linguistic shifts over generations.

The history of Reca is defined by its founder, Recê Çûmezin. Oral traditions state that Rec was the son of Çûmezin. Rec settled in the current village location, while his brothers migrated elsewhere; one brother established the Kurdî family in Sewliyê near Atme, and another named Hemê Recê settled near Recû. The village of Reca is home to three main families: Osê, Hemê Recê, and Wehîd. Additionally, the Şe'ro and Bekirê Reşo families migrated to Reca from Gûrda.

The landscape around the villages is rich with historical and natural landmarks. A stream known as Çemêreşkê, also called Geliyê Şêran, runs below the villages. Along this watercourse lies the ruin of an ancient water mill known as Aşê Elî Axa. To the south lies Gaza Zêra, an area noted for its olive trees and construction, and Çiyayê Eliyê Omê. The villagers historically relied on the Bîra Eliyê Carat well and the Kaniya Mîra spring. A significant historical site mentioned is a Roman era well located in Geliye Bîrê, which historically supplied water to seven surrounding villages including Me'serkê, Birqasê, and Çûmazna. Near this well stands a massive Çinar tree, estimated by locals to be 1300 years old, predating the era of Selahedîn.

Elder residents like Apê Remzî recall the French Mandate era, noting that local men were conscripted into the French army or worked in their outposts from Ezaz to Cerablûs. The region saw resistance from local figures such as Çûlaq and Mihdînê Îbşo, who fought against French forces. The village school, which served five surrounding villages, was notably closed in 1958. Despite administrative name changes imposed by successive governments, the villagers have retained their original Kurmanji names for their homes and landmarks.

II. Summary of Elcara - Mabeta from Afrin 366

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bsZnaYoU4bE

The village of Elîcaro, which is also referred to by the Arabic name Ancaro, is located in the Çiyayê Kurmênc region within the administrative jurisdiction of Mabato. It is situated approximately 10 kilometers away from the town of Mabato. The settlement is relatively small and contains about 40 households. The social structure is described as very cohesive, with residents referring to one another as cousins and stating that there are no strangers among them.

The transcript identifies several specific families and households that make up the community. The video documentation was requested by Ciwan Ehmed Elî, who is associated with the house of Ebû Selah. Other specific households mentioned include the families of Mihemed Îbo and Mele Xelîl 'Ezîz, although the speakers note that these particular families were not present in the village at the time of filming. Historical continuity is evident in the architecture, with specific mention of the Hewşa Ebû Reşîdê Şêx and Hewşa Bavkê. One notable structure is referred to as Hewşa Kavlorê, where inscriptions date the construction or renovation to 1960 and 1977.

Daily life in Elîcaro revolves around agriculture, specifically olive farming. Residents such as Ebû Şadî have worked in the olive groves for decades, performing tasks like kesaẍê or pruning. The village relies on external sources for some provisions. There is no local bakery, so bread is brought in from Mabato or the nearby village of Hisêr. Water is managed through cisterns known as Birkê or delivered by trucks. The village contains a primary school, referred to as the Mekteb, which provides education for children up to the sixth grade.

The geography surrounding Elîcaro includes several neighboring villages and landmarks. Directly opposite lies the village of Berko, and nearby is the smaller hamlet of Me'serkê, which consists of about 20 households. Other neighboring locations include Indîdarê, Raco, Tirmîşa, Çoman, and Mayê. Within the village boundaries, there are remnants of the past, including a significant tree called Darê jiyê and ancient stone olive presses referred to as Deryûb or Gilê. The speakers also mention a ruined site called a Meqbez, which served as an old cemetery or religious site but is now in disrepair.

The village of Elî Caro gets its name from an ancestor named Eliyê Jare. He had four sons who founded the main lineages of the village, including the Hecbirîm, Os, Ehmed, and Şêx families. Guests also mentioned the Hesûn and Eme families. The narrator notes that their roots trace back to the Biyan tribe. Today Elî Caro consists of roughly sixty houses.

An elderly resident named Remzî provides a fascinating historical account of his time serving in the French military during the mandate period. He served for four years in areas like Ezaz, Çobanbeg, and Cerablus. He recalls the regional conflicts involving Çolox and Mihedînê Îbşaşî against the French forces. To suppress Çolox, the French utilized Kurdish cavalry squadrons, noting the bravery of the Kurdish fighters. Other notable local figures from that era included Ehmed Şêxo from Qurtqilaqê and Reşîdê Îbo. Later, during the Hafiz Esed era in the nineteen seventies, Remzî served as the mukhtar for six surrounding villages. He notes that the Syrian state repeatedly changed the village names to Arabic forms like Mahsere and Misteşûra, although the locals retained their original Kurdish names.

The villages are built on a mountain called Gêyaza Zêra and are surrounded by Çiyayê Eiyê Omê. A prominent historical landmark is a massive Çinar tree, estimated to be thirteen hundred years old, and a historic well known as Bîra Elî Carat situated in the Geliyê Bîrê valley. Historically, this well provided water to seven neighboring villages. Agriculture, specifically olive cultivation, is the primary livelihood. A local carpenter named Xelîl details how he used to craft agricultural tools like threshing sledges and wooden spoons from Qetlebe wood, reflecting the deep agricultural traditions of the community.

II. Summary of Elcara - Mabeta from Multi Channel

The documentary explores the adjacent villages of Êlî Caro and Raca in the Mabeta district of the Afrin region. Over the years, these two settlements have merged into a single community separated only by a central mosque. The residents share a primary school, a cemetery, and a local commune, functioning essentially as one large village.

The name Êlî Caro comes from an early settler named Eliyê Jaro, who had four sons: Hec Birîm, Os, Ehmed, and Şêx. The village expanded steadily through their descendants. The families living in the villages today descend primarily from these original founders. In Êlî Caro, the prominent lineages include the families of Hesên, Hec Birîm, Eme, Ehmed, and Şêx.

The surrounding landscape features several significant historical landmarks. A prominent site located in the Geliyê Bîrê valley is Bîra Omer, an ancient Roman well that local residents believe to be over a thousand years old. Beside this well stands an equally ancient plane tree known locally as darê çinarê. Another important water source is Bîra Alîceran, which provides a steady supply of water throughout the entire year. The geography around the villages also includes the Birc Qaşan ruins to the north, the Şiketa area to the west, and the towering Çiyayê Eloyê Omê mountain to the south.

The region has a rich history of resilience and adaptation. An elder named Remzî recounts his four years of service in the French military during the mandate period, where he was stationed in areas such as Cerablus, Ezaz, and Çobanbeg. The villagers proudly remember the fierce Kurdish resistance against French forces, led by legendary local figures like Miheddînê Îbşoşê and Çolaq. Çolaq fought bravely until he was ultimately killed in battle at Topraqê Çeqel. The French commanders were so impressed by the bravery of the local fighters that they established a special squadron named eskaderon Kurd. The elder also notes how the Syrian state repeatedly altered the Kurdish names of surrounding villages like Êlî Caro and Me'serkê, continuing a long legacy of cultural erasure and renaming by various ruling powers.

Culturally, the villagers maintain a strong communal bond. An elder carpenter named Xelîl keeps traditional crafts alive by handcrafting wooden agricultural tools like the cercer and various household utensils. Residents also reminisce about the transition from communal mud houses with open hearths to modern living, noting that traditional wood stoves remain absolutely essential for surviving the harsh mountain winters.

II. Summary of Elcara - Mabeta from Multi Channel 2

The documentary explores the connected villages of Elî Cara and Raca, situated in the Mabetan district of the Afrin region, roughly fifteen kilometers west of Mabeta. Built on Çiyayê Gêza, a mountain formerly covered in pine forests, these two villages have effectively merged into a single community over the years. They are separated only by a central mosque and share a school, a cemetery, and a commune named after Şehîd Xoşnav.

The history and naming of both villages trace back to their initial founders. Elî Cara is named after Elîyê Carê, who is also known as Elî Carû in Arab dialects. He had four sons named Hecbirîm, Os, Ehmed, and Şêx, whose descendants make up the primary population. The lineage of Elî Cara belongs to the Biyan tribe.

Today, Elî Cara has about sixty houses, while the combined population of both villages is over one thousand people across approximately one hundred and thirty homes. Distinct families reside here. In Elî Cara, the prominent families include Hesûn, Hecbirîm, Eme, Ehmed, and Şêx.

The region is marked by significant events during the French mandate period. Apê Remzî, an elder over eighty years old who has served as the mukhtar of six surrounding villages for forty five years, shares his military past. He fled from Turkey as a teenager and served the French forces for four years in areas like Ezzaz, Çobanbeg, and Cerablus. During this time, the French military created a Kurdish squadron known as Eskaderona Kurd or Lêcê Kurd. Meanwhile, Kurdish rebels actively resisted the occupation. Mehmed Dînê Îbşoşê, also known as Mehe Îbşoşê, was one of the first to fight the French forces. Another prominent rebel, Çolaq, fought fiercely but was eventually ambushed from behind by soldiers arriving from Încirniyê and was killed near a hill in Kêmraşê. Çolaq had a son named Evdo who was also killed during these conflicts. Other individuals like Reşîd Îbo are remembered from this era, while locals such as Ehmed Şêxo from Qitmê and Bedrî Biraq from Qirqilaxê Mezin served in the mandate forces.

Over time, the villagers endured state assimilation attempts, notably when the Syrian government altered their traditional names. Elî Cara was officially renamed El Hîcarû, and the neighboring village of Mehsercik was changed to Mehsera. However, the community preserved its original identities. The villagers also recall the historic ancient well located in Geliyê Bîrê, which once supplied water to seven surrounding settlements, including Mehserkê, Birka, Çumezna, Raco, Mistheşura, and Hec Qasim, before modern water access was established.

II. Summary of Elcara - Mabeta from Multi Channel 3

The documentary explores the historical and cultural landscape of several communities in the Efrînê region, beginning with a detailed look at the village of Hawcê. A central focal point in Hawcê is an ancient plane tree known as Dara Ne'cîkê, which stands alongside a historic valley called Geliyê Bîrê. In this valley lies an ancient well referred to as Bîra Romaniyan because locals trace its origins back to the Roman period. Villagers estimate the well and the adjacent plane tree to be around 1300 years old, noting that they existed long before the time of Selahedîn. Historically, people would bring their livestock to this valley for water, and the well is famous for never running dry, even supplying water throughout the spring months for recreation and community picnics. The narrator also notes that to the west of Hawcê lies another water source called Bîra Alîcaratê, which has continually served the neighboring communities and their flocks.

The village of Hawcê is deeply rooted in traditional agriculture and crafts. An elder named Xelîl shares memories of his life as a self taught carpenter. He recalls the challenging but highly communal times before modern machinery, when villagers relied entirely on animals for plowing and transporting goods. He describes making wooden threshing sledges, known locally as cencer, which were essential for processing grain. The elder reminisces about how villagers used to cooperate extensively during the harvest season, sharing tools and labor until every single family finished their work. He also describes the manual creation of olive and grape presses. When discussing the heavy craftsmanship of the past, the conversation highlights how people from the nearby village of Kûranê, specifically the Xelîfe and Ebdî families, used to carve basalt stone to make agricultural implements, sourcing the dark stone from Çiyayê Reş.

Geographically, the documentary outlines the borders and neighboring settlements in the immediate area. South of the area are the village of Mist'eşûra and the mountain of Çiyayê Eliyê Ome. To the north lies Bircikê, to the east is the close settlement of Çomêzina, and to the west is the village of Şêxêtiyan.
